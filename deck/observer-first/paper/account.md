# Algorithmic Idealism, the plain account

Physics usually asks what is the case in the world and treats observers as objects inside it. Markus P. Müller reverses the order. He takes as fundamental the first-person question of what an observer will experience next ([the reversal](record:080f095a-9d6b-412e-ae3f-c15feb94e3df)). An observer at a moment is only an abstract pattern, a self state, which in the simplest model is a finite string of bits ([self state](record:30da47c3-b67a-4a4f-b6c9-f5ab0d19bf87)). The one law is that the chance of becoming one next state rather than another is fixed by universal induction on the present state: in the "bit model" this is Solomonoff's algorithmic probability, which gives more weight to continuations that a short computer program would produce ([the postulate](record:9cbf5647-ce05-401f-8910-abd6821c271d)). These chances are objective, not anyone's beliefs, and no underlying world causes them.

From that starting point the papers derive the world. Regularities in an observer's past tend to persist ([persistence](record:b634172d-deff-4377-9f7e-3639e893005d)), and with high probability the observer comes to see itself as embedded in an external world run by simple, computable, probabilistic laws ([emergent world](record:de0df8b3-968f-41c3-b7db-755819a13125)). Two observers who have each seen enough come to agree on one world, so objective reality is a theorem that holds asymptotically, not an assumption ([objective reality](record:bc2f5a20-7e64-4140-9824-e49cbba2ab41)). Simple initial conditions, a low-entropy past, follow from the preference for short programs ([low entropy](record:6d87beff-602e-4487-a74d-6afba2b40bbd)). Time, in the sense of "now" and "next", is a first-person ordering, and spacetime is one convenient model among many that would give the same predictions.

Where the external picture breaks down, the framework gives answers physics alone does not. An observer should never bet on being a Boltzmann brain, however many there are ([Boltzmann brains](record:7c13f73c-7464-4b89-a7f4-03724c979397)). Shutting down a computer simulation does not, in general, end the experience of the agents simulated in it ([simulation](record:c608a2fe-c455-4b06-a9a1-33957f1adc48)). A young or simple observer seen in another's world need not match its own first-person chances, a "probabilistic changeling" ([changelings](record:0bbd9ea4-ec5a-4a35-b5b9-053d59550910)). Müller and Caroline Jones trace these puzzles, with extended Wigner's friend scenarios, to one feature they call Restriction A: a physical theory cannot give a probabilistic description of the observations of all agents ([Restriction A](record:6592dae1-c429-4404-9a55-2ae6c5c93676)).

The quantum measurement problem dissolves in this view, since quantum states are an observer's states of knowledge and the update rule is Bayesian updating ([measurement](record:0a54b4c1-9571-4e6e-98ba-aaa64592ccb4)). Consciousness plays no fundamental role: only a small subset of self states would describe a conscious observer ([consciousness](record:a30c7534-0b76-4f1a-9418-8b8d6c0a9abc)). Müller counts the program incomplete. It cannot yet treat forgetting, and its consistency with physics in the laboratory rests on a physical Church-Turing thesis. Its ability to reconstruct genuinely quantum statistics, such as Bell violation without signalling, is named as the test still ahead.
